# Investing Is the Easy Part

I did not become an index investor because I was born humble.

I became one after losing money in individual stocks.

There is a familiar sequence. First you buy a few companies because the story sounds obvious. Then you read more. Then you believe you are no longer a beginner. You see other people being careless, and this is dangerous, because it makes your own carelessness look like analysis. You are not gambling. You are building a thesis.

Sometimes the thesis even works. That is worse. A bad stock that loses money quickly teaches a clean lesson. A stock that makes money for the wrong reason teaches the wrong lesson with confidence.

For a while, I believed I could beat the index.

I no longer think this is impossible. Some people can. A few investors have the rare mix of skill, temperament, and process that survives more than one market cycle.

I just no longer assume that person is me.

I also do not assume that person is most professional fund managers after fees. The evidence is not kind to that assumption either.[1](#note-1) That is a useful starting point. If investing is already difficult for people who do it full time, a household pursuing FI should be careful before making stock selection the engine of the plan.

The engine was hard enough to build.

The first twelve chapters of this book were about producing surplus. Work becomes optional only when a household keeps enough of its income, keeps fixed costs low, uses Singapore's system well, and refuses to turn fear into recurring expenses. All of that work creates a monthly gap between income and spending.

That gap is precious.

The job of investing is not to make the household feel clever. The job is to receive the surplus without destroying it.

## The Boring Machine

Investing is the easy part only if you let it stay boring.

This sounds like a slogan. It is closer to plumbing. Money arrives every month. Some of it must leave for the life. The rest needs a pipe into ownership: broad, low-cost, diversified ownership that can run for decades without requiring the household to win an argument with the market every quarter.

The old boring principles are still the useful ones. Know the goal. Diversify. Keep costs low. Stay disciplined.[2](#note-2)

None of this sounds like a secret. That is the point.

The household does not need a brilliant machine. It needs a machine that keeps working when the owner is tired, busy, overconfident, frightened, or distracted by a new story. A machine that only works when you are calm is just a mood.

This is where broad index investing earns its place.

An index fund does not promise to be the best investment. It promises something more useful for most households: to stop pretending the household must identify the best investment before the surplus is allowed to compound.

There will always be a better stock somewhere. There will always be a manager who just had a great year. There will always be a theme that makes an ordinary global index look dull.

Good.

The purpose of the default is not to win every comparison. The purpose is to keep the household in the game without making the game the household's second job.

## Humility Is A Return Enhancer

Humility sounds moral. In investing, it is practical.

If I believe I can reliably beat the market, I will concentrate, trade, wait for perfect entry points, and invent reasons why my current idea is different from everyone else's current idea. If I believe I probably cannot, I will do something less exciting: own the market broadly, keep buying, and spend my attention on the parts of FI I actually control.

That does not make active investing stupid. It makes active investing non-default.

There is a difference.

A person may enjoy analysing companies. A person may have a small account for individual stocks. A person may run a careful active strategy with clear rules and honest measurement. The problem begins when a household quietly lets that hobby become the plan that must work for FI to work.

FI should not depend on the household being above average at a zero-sum professional game.

This is why SPIVA-style scorecards matter. They are not there to win internet arguments about active versus passive investing. They are a reminder that many professional active funds underperform their benchmarks after fees over measured periods. In the U.S. large-cap category, for example, the 2025 SPIVA scorecard reported that 79 percent of active large-cap funds underperformed the S&P 500 that year.[1](#note-1) The exact number changes by market, category, and horizon. The lesson is simpler: beating the index is hard enough that it should not be the ordinary household's required skill.

The ordinary household has better work to do.

It can spend its energy on the real FI levers: savings rate, housing, daily cost, and the habits that keep surplus alive. Then it can invest the surplus in a broad machine and go live.

## Fees Are A Leak You Can Control

Most investment variables are slippery.

Next year's return, interest rates, and the winning market are all unknown. The next panic is unknown too, though people will explain it very confidently after it has happened.

Cost is not unknown.

Every layer of investment cost reduces the amount that remains in the household's account. A higher-cost product must perform better just to leave the investor in the same place after fees.[3](#note-3)

This is the same logic as the rest of the book.

A household cannot control gross salary perfectly. It can control some of the spending leaks. It cannot control market return. It can control some of the fee leaks.

Low fees will not make a bad plan good. But high fees can make a good asset class disappointing. They are the investment version of a quiet monthly subscription: small enough to ignore, large enough to matter over decades.

The boring machine should be cheap.

## DCA Is Not Magic

Dollar-cost averaging is easy to explain and hard to do.

The explanation is familiar: invest a fixed amount at regular intervals. When prices are high, that amount buys fewer units. When prices are low, it buys more. Over time, the household stops making one giant decision and starts making a repeated one.

There is no magic in that.

If markets rise over the period, investing a lump sum immediately often produces a better result than holding cash and feeding it in slowly. Vanguard's research has made that point clearly: cost averaging can reduce the regret of bad timing, but lump-sum investing has often produced higher wealth because cash left aside misses expected market exposure.[4](#note-4)

So why use DCA?

Because households are not spreadsheets wearing courage.

When markets make new highs, buying feels like chasing. When markets fall, buying feels like being stupid on schedule. The same person who calmly agreed with a long-term plan in January can become a market historian, economist, geopolitical analyst, and coward by March.

This is where automation matters.

My advice is not "be brave every month." That is a bad system. My advice is to remove as many monthly acts of bravery as possible. Set the recurring investment. Let salary arrive. Let the transfer happen. Let the broad fund be bought before the household has turned the market into a personal drama.

DCA is behaviour design.

It does not guarantee better returns. It increases the chance that the household actually invests the surplus instead of waiting for a clean emotional moment that never arrives.

## My Own Boring Machine

My own portfolio is probably more cautious than many believers in one-fund global equity investing would choose.

The core is still index-heavy: mostly global and U.S. equity index funds. Around the edges I hold small Singapore and Hong Kong index exposure, some precious metals, a little Bitcoin, and cash for rebalancing or adding during bad markets.[6](#note-6)

These are tickers, not instructions.

Nobody should copy this allocation just because it is mine. This is my system, built around my own risk tolerance, my own belief in diversification, my own desire to keep buying through unpleasant markets, and my own need for some dry powder. Purity is not the test. Following it is.

That matters more than it sounds.

A theoretically perfect portfolio that causes panic selling is worse than an imperfect portfolio that survives. The best investment plan is not the one that looks most elegant in a forum comment. It is the one that can pass through bad markets and messy family years without asking the owner to become a different person.

This is why I like the spirit of Nick Maggiulli's *Just Keep Buying* and Morgan Housel's *The Psychology of Money*.[5](#note-5) One pushes the accumulation habit. The other keeps returning to behaviour, humility, risk, and survival. I do not read either as a formula. I read them as reminders that the hard part of investing is usually not multiplication. It is continuing to act sensibly when your own brain would prefer a story.

## The Singapore Layer

A Singapore household has one more reason to keep this chapter simple.

CPF already gives many households a defensive SGD floor with gates attached: compulsory, local-currency wealth with interest-rate features that behave, in part, like a conservative balance-sheet anchor. It is still not cash, a liquid bond fund, or a complete portfolio.

That distinction matters.

CPF can reduce the need to make every outside-CPF dollar defensive. For many households, the investable portfolio outside CPF needs to do more of the growth work. But that does not mean the answer is "all global equities, no questions asked." A household still needs liquidity, tax awareness, and a portfolio it can hold.

Those details matter. They just do not belong in this chapter.

We will get to the Singapore-specific complications later: local indices, REITs, tax jurisdiction, CPF/SRS rules, and the strange fact that two products both called "index funds" can behave very differently.

For now, the method is enough.

Build a machine that is broad enough, cheap enough, automatic enough, and boring enough to survive a bad year.

Then keep feeding it.

## The Practical Rule

Do not ask the investment plan to save a household that refuses to produce surplus.

That was the point of the first twelve chapters. The household first has to keep money. It has to know its numbers. It has to avoid turning income into status, convenience, anxiety, and lifestyle machinery.

After that, investing has a humbler job.

It receives the surplus.

It should be diversified enough that one company, one market, or one theme cannot ruin the plan. It should be boring enough to keep running through highs, lows, and ordinary family life. Low cost and automation are not decoration; they are what keep the machine from leaking or stalling.

The household does not need to become an investing hero.

It needs to stop interrupting compounding.

That sounds small. Over decades, it is most of the game.

The next chapter asks a question that boring investors still need to answer: when you say "buy the index," which index do you mean, and through which wrapper? STI, S&P 500, and MSCI World are not the same machine. The fund around the index matters too. That is [Chapter 14: The Index Is Not the Product](chapter-14.md).
