# The Index Is Not the Product

"Just buy the index" sounds like an answer.

For a Singapore investor, it is the beginning of two questions.

Which index?

And through which wrapper?

The first question decides what you own. The second decides how you own it. People often collapse the two into one word: ETF, fund, index, passive. That shortcut is convenient. It is also how a household can think it bought one simple thing and later discover it bought several hidden exposures at once.

Imagine a Singapore investor who wants a boring S&P 500 fund.

The economic idea sounds simple: own five hundred large American companies. The fund name may even look simple. But the product still has a domicile, a listing venue, an income treatment, a currency quotation, and a liquidity profile. Some of those differences are cosmetic. Some change tax drag, estate exposure, liquidity, and how painful the paperwork may be for the family later.

The index is not the product.

The index tells you the bet. The wrapper decides how that bet reaches the household.

## STI Is A Local Bet

Start with the most familiar index.

The Straits Times Index feels like "Singapore stocks." In one sense, it is. Local names, Singapore-dollar quotation, and relatively clean local tax treatment make it useful for some investors. Singapore does not generally tax individual investors on one-tier local dividends or ordinary capital gains from personal investment activity.[1](#note-1)

That is real.

But STI is not Singapore Inc.

It is a small headline index, and it is very top-heavy. In the FTSE Russell factsheet for 30 April 2026, the top ten holdings made up 78.33 percent of the index. DBS alone was 25.96 percent. DBS, OCBC, and UOB together were 51.36 percent. Banks were 51.36 percent of the index; real estate was another 15.38 percent.[2](#note-2)

So when a household buys STI, the package is narrower than the label suggests: Singapore banks, local property exposure, and a few other large local names.

That package can be attractive. The same factsheet showed a 4.08 percent dividend yield and strong recent total returns: 34.0 percent over twelve months and 14.0 percent annualised over five years.[2](#note-2) The lazy old line that STI never moves is not good enough. Recent data can embarrass lazy opinions.

The better point is narrower.

STI may be a useful local-income tool. It is not a global growth engine. For a Singapore household, it may also repeat risks already in the life: salary, CPF, housing, school, career. Buying more Singapore banks may still be rational. It should be named as more Singapore exposure, not disguised as neutral diversification.

Home bias feels safe because the names are familiar.

Familiar is not the same as diversified.

## S&P 500 Is Not The World

The opposite mistake is to treat the S&P 500 as "the market."

It is a magnificent index, full of companies that have become part of modern capitalism's operating system. Many earn revenue globally. For a Singapore investor, a simple S&P 500 fund can be cleaner, broader, and more growth-oriented than a narrow local portfolio.

Still, the S&P 500 is not the world.

S&P Dow Jones Indices describes it as a U.S. large-cap gauge covering about 80 percent of available U.S. market capitalisation.[3](#note-3) In the April 2026 factsheet, the country weight was 100 percent United States. The top ten companies were 38.5 percent of the index.[3](#note-3)

That does not make it bad.

It makes it specific.

Buying the S&P 500 is a bet that U.S. large-cap capitalism remains an excellent default. It may be a good bet. It has been a very good bet for long stretches. But it is not the same as buying global capitalism. It excludes smaller U.S. companies, most non-U.S. companies, and the possibility that the next thirty years look less like the last fifteen.

In Ch13, the argument for index investing was humility. Do not assume you can pick the winning company. The same humility can be extended one level up: do not assume you can pick the winning country forever.

That is where global indexing enters.

## Global Is A Humility Default

A global index is not magic either.

MSCI ACWI, for example, covered 23 developed markets and 24 emerging markets in its April 2026 factsheet, with 2,514 constituents and about 85 percent coverage of the global investable equity opportunity set.[4](#note-4) That is far broader than STI and broader by country than the S&P 500.

But it is not some perfectly equal map of the world.

The same factsheet showed a U.S. weight of 63.41 percent, with the top ten holdings at 24.62 percent.[4](#note-4) Global market-cap weighting does not eliminate America. It stops you from deciding, on your own, that America must always be 100 percent of the answer.

That is a different kind of humility.

The global index investor is not saying, "The world index will beat the S&P 500." Maybe it will not. The claim is more modest: "I do not know which country will dominate my investing lifetime, so I will not make that forecast the foundation of FI."

This is why the index matters.

STI, S&P 500, and ACWI are all passive in the narrow sense. They are not passive in what they expose the household to. One concentrates in local banks and property. One concentrates in U.S. large caps. One spreads the decision across global market weights, while still inheriting the world's current U.S. tilt.

Passive does not mean neutral.

It means the rules are written in advance.

## The Wrapper Changes The Result

Now comes the second question.

Suppose the household has chosen the exposure. It wants U.S. equities, or global equities. It knows why. The next question is how to hold that exposure.

This is where Singapore investors often get surprised.

Singapore's local tax environment is friendly to personal investors. There is generally no capital gains tax on ordinary personal investment gains. Estate duty has been abolished since 15 February 2008.[1](#note-1) Local one-tier dividends are generally not taxed again in the hands of individual shareholders.

It is easy to mentally export that friendliness to every product bought from a brokerage account.

That is a mistake.

U.S.-source dividends paid to foreign persons are generally subject to 30 percent withholding unless reduced by treaty.[5](#note-5) Singapore does not have the kind of U.S. tax treaty that reduces that dividend withholding rate for ordinary portfolio investors. A U.S.-domiciled ETF holding dividend-paying U.S. stocks can be efficient in fees and liquidity, but the dividend leak is real.

There is also an estate angle. The IRS requires Form 706-NA filing for nonresident non-citizen estates where U.S.-situated assets exceed US$60,000, and U.S.-law corporation stock is generally U.S.-situated property.[6](#note-6) This is not the same as saying every Singapore investor's family will definitely pay a large U.S. estate tax bill. Actual outcomes depend on the estate, credits, treaties, structure, and facts. The practical point is enough: a U.S.-domiciled fund can create exposure and paperwork that the index name does not show.

The wrapper is not small print.

It is part of the FI result.

## Ireland Is Common, Not Magic

This is why Ireland-domiciled UCITS ETFs are common among Singapore investors who want U.S. or global equity exposure.

The usual logic is simple. A U.S.-domiciled ETF receives U.S. dividends and distributes them to a Singapore resident, typically with 30 percent U.S. withholding. An Ireland-domiciled fund holding U.S. equities usually benefits from the U.S.-Ireland treaty rate at the fund level, commonly 15 percent for portfolio dividends.[7](#note-7) If the fund is accumulating, the remaining dividend is reinvested inside the fund rather than paid out to the investor.

That does not erase the 15 percent withholding.

It just means the wrapper often reduces the U.S. dividend drag versus direct U.S.-domiciled exposure, and it avoids the same direct holding of U.S.-domiciled ETF shares that can raise U.S. situs estate concerns.

Useful, yes. Magic, no.

Ireland-domiciled funds can have higher expense ratios, different liquidity, wider spreads, different trading hours, and more platform friction. A fund may trade in USD, GBP, or SGD, but listing currency is not the same as underlying currency exposure. Unless a share class is explicitly hedged, changing the quote currency does not make the underlying companies stop being global equities.

The same warning applies to listing venue.

Listed in Singapore does not automatically mean Singapore-domiciled. Listed in London does not automatically mean U.K.-domiciled. Domicile is the legal home of the fund. Listing venue is where you trade it. For tax and estate questions, the legal home often matters more than the exchange screen.

This is the level of detail the household needs.

Not every operational detail. Not every ticker. Not every broker route. Enough to know that "I bought the S&P 500" is not a complete sentence.

## Local Convenience Has A Price Too

Local products can be attractive for a different reason.

Local convenience has real value because assets must be understandable, findable, and administrable by the family, not only optimised by the investor. For some investors, administrative simplicity is not a small thing. A perfect product that nobody in the family can find after death is not perfect.

The old personal-finance mistake is to optimise only expected return.

The older-family mistake is to ignore paperwork.

Still, convenience has its own price. A local or SGX-listed product may have lower liquidity, wider spreads, smaller asset size, different tracking quality, or fewer fund choices. It may also give a different exposure from the one the investor thinks he is buying. Again, the question is not whether local is good or bad. The question is what exposure and wrapper the household is actually choosing.

A Singapore investor should be allowed to prefer simplicity.

He should not confuse simplicity with free.

## The Practical Rule

Before buying an index fund, ask two questions in order.

First, what does this thing own?

That question catches the STI problem and the S&P 500 problem. STI may be more local-bank-and-property than the name suggests. S&P 500 may be more U.S.-large-cap than the word "market" suggests. A global index may still be U.S.-heavy, but at least it makes the country bet explicit.

Second, where does this fund live?

That question catches the wrapper problem. The fund's domicile, dividend treatment, estate exposure, costs, liquidity, and administrative friction can change what the household actually keeps.

Do not begin with the ticker.

Begin with the ownership. Then inspect the wrapper. If both make sense, the fund can be part of the boring machine. If either is misunderstood, the machine may still run, but it is not as boring as it looks.

The index chooses the bet.

The wrapper decides how much of the bet survives.

Once the household knows what it owns and how it owns it, the next question becomes more personal: how much of this machine is enough for the life it is meant to fund? That is [Chapter 15: How Much Is Enough?](chapter-15.md).
