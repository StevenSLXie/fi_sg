# The First Convenience Tax

> **Navigation:** [← Why We Left the Condo](chapter-09.md) · [Contents](../SUMMARY.md) · [The Renaissance Household →](chapter-11.md)

A meal is one thing. A food bill is several things stacked together.

The first layer is ingredients. The second is someone else's kitchen. The third is food court convenience. The fourth is the restaurant ritual, with service charge and GST arriving after the menu has done its advertising.

Singapore makes this stack unusually visible. In the same city, sometimes on the same street, a household can buy the same basic function in four ways.

| Food channel | What the household is buying | FI reading |
|---|---|---|
| Groceries and home cooking | Ingredients, plus household time and tools | Lowest cash layer when the system is simple |
| Hawker centre / kopitiam | Low-cost outsourced cooking | Good pressure valve for time and energy |
| Mall food court | Outsourced cooking plus rent and comfort | Useful, but usually a higher layer |
| Restaurant | Food plus service, space, ritual, and bill mechanics | Fine as an occasion, dangerous as routine |

The mistake is treating all four as "food".

Each solves a different problem. A tired parent buying chicken rice at a kopitiam is buying a practical service. A couple celebrating an anniversary at a restaurant is buying a ritual. A household ordering restaurant meals three times a week because nobody designed a default dinner system is buying convenience as a recurring tax.

That last category is the one that matters for financial independence.

FI does not require a miserable diet. It requires the household to know which layer it is buying.

## The Average Food Budget Buys Service

The Singapore data is useful because it says something most residents already know.

In the 2023 Household Expenditure Survey, the average household spent S$5,931 a month before imputed owner-occupied rent, or S$7,119 including it. On the including-imputed-rent presentation, housing, food, and transport together made up 63.2 percent of spending. Food was S$1,422 a month, or 20.0 percent on that basis.[1](#note-1)

But the split inside food is more interesting than the headline number.

Average monthly spending on food and non-alcoholic beverages, the grocery-like category, was S$456. Spending on food and beverage serving services was S$966. In other words, about two-thirds of the average food budget was not buying ingredients. It was buying someone else to cook, serve, rent the space, clean up, and keep the business alive.

That is not a moral failure. It is the way Singapore is built.

The city has a dense everyday food system: hawker centres, kopitiams, food courts, restaurants, delivery platforms, and supermarkets all overlap. For a working adult, eating out is often the default because the system makes it easy.

Easy is valuable.

Easy is also billable.

The FI question is not "Do you eat out?" The FI question is "Which part of eating out has become automatic?"

## Singapore's Cheap Outsourced Kitchen

Hawker centres are one of Singapore's underrated household-finance institutions.

NEA says it manages 123 markets and hawker centres, and describes them as places where people can enjoy affordable food in a clean and hygienic environment.[2](#note-2) That is a dry public-agency sentence. Underneath it sits a real household advantage: Singapore has a low-cost outsourced kitchen available to ordinary residents.

For a family without a helper or grandparents nearby, this matters. Cooking is not only money. It is planning, heat, dishes, and the question every household eventually hates: what are we eating tonight?

Hawker centres and kopitiams absorb part of that load. They convert household time and energy into a few dollars per meal. In many countries, the cheap alternative to home cooking is fast food or poor-quality takeaway. In Singapore, the cheap alternative can be fish soup, cai fan, ban mian, nasi padang, chicken rice, yong tau foo, or a packet of economic bee hoon before work.

That is a real advantage.

The Institute of Policy Studies' Makan Index 2.0 surveyed hawker centres, food courts, and kopitiams across residential neighbourhoods in 2022 and early 2023. It estimated an average three-meal day at S$16.89 per person across these everyday eating-out places, with food courts generally more expensive than kopitiams and hawker centres.[3](#note-3)

The exact price changes by neighbourhood, stall, year, portion, and appetite. The pattern is more important: "eating out" is not one price.

A hawker centre is not the same as an air-conditioned mall food court. A kopitiam under an HDB block is not the same as a restaurant in a mall. A strata-titled old mall with cheap rent can hide stalls that would not survive in a polished retail complex. A five-minute walk can move the household from one cost layer to another.

Sometimes the cheaper lunch is five minutes away.

You do not need a relocation plan for that. You only need to stop buying lunch inside the most expensive building on the route between office and MRT.

## Food Court Is Not The Bottom

The Singapore food system can make a household too comfortable with the middle layer.

Hawker and kopitiam meals are cheap compared with restaurants. Food courts are often still cheaper than restaurants. But the lowest cash layer is usually not food court. It is buying ingredients and cooking at home, especially for repeatable meals built around protein, vegetables, eggs, rice, noodles, or soup.

Home cooking removes more of the service layer.

Each layer adds rent, labour, and margin. Food courts add mall costs. Restaurants add service, idle capacity, GST administration, and sometimes a 10 percent service charge before GST.[5](#note-5)

The household does not remove every cost. It pays with time, mental load, utilities, equipment, and planning. But if the system is simple enough, the cash arithmetic can be very hard to ignore.

Take a basic protein-and-vegetable dinner. It does not need to win a cooking competition. It only needs to feed the family.

The Makan Index dinner estimate for everyday eating-out places was S$6.20 per person in the 2022 / early-2023 survey. Four prepared dinners would therefore be about S$24.80 before drinks, extras, delivery, or any later price changes.[3](#note-3)

Now compare a plain grocery basket. In a May 2026 online supermarket check, one basic four-person dinner basket could be assembled from frozen chicken breast, eggs or tofu, leafy vegetables, rice, and pantry seasoning for about S$14-18, depending on the exact brands, portions, promotions, and pantry assumptions.[4](#note-4)

The example is not a recipe and not a law of nature. Fresh meat, fish, organic vegetables, premium supermarkets, food waste, small households, and tired adults can change the answer. But it shows why the gap widens with household size. Four prepared meals require four stall margins. One pot of soup, one steamed chicken, one tray of roasted vegetables, or one rice-cooker meal can feed several people from a single basket.

That is why the "food court is cheap" argument is only half complete.

Food court is cheap compared with restaurant routine. Home cooking is cheaper because the household strips out still more of the paid service. For a single person working late, the savings may not justify the effort every night. For a family of four eating repeated dinners, packed lunches, or weekend meals, the difference can become a serious monthly number.

The old-fashioned sentence still works: buy meat and vegetables, cook them yourself, and the bill falls a lot.

The sentence becomes useless when it turns into a lecture. A good parent does not need to cook every meal. A household pursuing FI does need a cheaper default than mall rent.

## The Meal Should Be Simple

Home cooking fails when the household tries to import restaurant ambition into weekday dinner.

Elaborate cooking is a hobby. Daily cooking is infrastructure.

The infrastructure version is boring on purpose. It uses repeatable defaults: a few proteins, a few vegetables, a staple carb, and tools that reduce friction. The dishwasher, rice cooker, freezer, or air fryer matter only if they make the cheap default easier to repeat.

The goal is not to make every meal memorable.

The goal is to make the default meal easy enough that the household does not surrender to restaurant spending because chopping garlic feels like a second job.

My own default is not glamorous. Steam or roast a protein. Boil or stir-fry a vegetable. Add rice, noodles, potatoes, fruit, or soup. Repeat with minor variations. A meal like that is not Instagram content. That is part of the attraction. Instagram content is rarely optimised for savings rate.

The health side is secondary, but real. Singapore's National Population Health Survey materials cite National Nutrition Survey 2022 findings that about nine in ten residents exceeded the recommended sodium limit, with average sodium intake around 3,620mg a day. Hypertension prevalence rose from 19.8 percent in 2010 to 37.0 percent in 2021-2022.[6](#note-6)

Home cooking does not automatically make a household healthy. A person can deep-fry at home with great enthusiasm. But home cooking gives control over salt, oil, portion size, carbs, and protein. That control has financial value too. Health problems are not only medical issues. They are time issues, energy issues, insurance issues, and eventually FI issues.

Still, health should not be used as a stick.

The main chapter argument is cash flow. If a household can make ordinary meals easy, the restaurant stops being the emergency backup for every tired evening.

## Restaurants Are For Occasions

Restaurants are not evil. They are just expensive at the exact thing FI cares about: recurring default spending.

A restaurant meal can be worth every dollar. It can mark a birthday, restore a marriage after a bad week, host visiting parents, close a friendship gap, or give the household a night where nobody shops, cooks, cleans, or negotiates with a child over broccoli. Those are real services.

But restaurant-as-routine and restaurant-as-occasion are different products.

Routine restaurant spending is dangerous because it enters the budget quietly. It rarely feels like one large decision. It arrives as a Friday dinner, then a Saturday lunch, then delivery because it is raining, then a cafe brunch, then a workday upgrade because the office area has better options, then a child who has learned that mall food is normal food.

Each decision can be defensible. The pattern is expensive.

Restaurant bills also hide their final price well. A S$14 menu item becomes about S$16.79 when a restaurant applies a 10 percent service charge and then 9 percent GST to the post-service amount. Service charge is not mandatory across all restaurants, but when it appears, the menu price is only a starting bid.

The arithmetic compounds at family scale. One S$80 restaurant meal a week is more than S$4,000 a year. At a 3.5 percent FI gauge, S$4,000 of recurring annual spending requires about S$114,000 more FI asset base.

`S$4,000 / 0.035 = about S$114,000`

Double the habit, and the required asset base doubles with it.

This does not mean "never go". It means the restaurant should be promoted from default to occasion. If the meal is genuinely social, celebratory, restorative, or excellent, buy it deliberately. If the restaurant is merely doing weekday logistics because the household never built a cheaper system, the bill is not entertainment. It is process failure with nicer lighting.

## The Practical Rule

Build a food ladder before hunger makes the decision.

At the bottom, keep a default home system: several simple meals the household can make without creativity. Stock the ingredients. Use tools. Accept repetition. Make the meal boring enough to survive workdays.

Above that, use hawker centres, kopitiams, and practical food-court meals as pressure valves. A household that cooks most meals does not need to feel guilty about outsourcing some meals. Singapore's cheap outsourced kitchen is part of the system advantage. Use it when time, childcare, commute, or fatigue makes the trade worth it.

Above that, treat restaurants as occasions, not operating expenses. They can still belong in a good FI life. They just should not be the household's default kitchen.

The rule is simple: buy the lowest layer that solves the real problem.

If the problem is calories and nutrition, groceries may solve it. If the problem is no time and no energy, a hawker centre may solve it. If the problem is a birthday, a date, a guest, or a memory, a restaurant may solve it. Trouble starts when every problem is solved at the restaurant layer because the lower layers were never designed.

Chapter 9 argued that private housing can turn shelter into a higher cost floor. Food repeats the same pattern in miniature. The household does not become poor from one restaurant meal. It becomes slower when convenience becomes the default answer to a daily question.

The next chapter widens that idea from meals to vendors. If food is the first convenience tax, the rest of the household has its own versions: technicians, cleaners, repairs, tools, and the practical skills that keep small problems from becoming permanent bills.
