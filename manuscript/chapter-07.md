# The Strong Dollar Under Your Feet

In 1988, one Singapore dollar bought about 1.30 Malaysian ringgit.

In 2025, it bought about 3.28.

That sentence explains a certain feeling Singaporeans have when they cross the Causeway. The taxi ride looks different. The grocery bill looks different. A plate of char kway teow looks cheap until every Singaporean in the queue has the same idea. The same salary, translated through a different currency, becomes a different life.

This is why the Singapore dollar belongs in a book about financial independence.

Most people think of currency only when they travel. They notice it at the money changer, on a credit-card statement, or when an overseas university invoice arrives and suddenly the family WhatsApp group becomes a foreign-exchange desk.

FI makes the same issue permanent. If your future spending is in Singapore dollars, your assets must eventually support a Singapore-dollar life. If some of your future life can happen outside Singapore, the Singapore dollar becomes a portable purchasing-power machine. If much of your portfolio is in U.S. or global assets, the currency layer sits quietly under the investment layer whether you look at it or not.

The money that survives Chapter 6 is not floating in the air. It is sitting on a currency.

For a Singapore household, that currency has been unusually strong. Not every year. Not against every currency. Not by divine right. But over a working life, the pattern is hard to ignore. The Singapore dollar has appreciated sharply against the Malaysian ringgit. Against the U.S. dollar, the story is more mixed by cycle, but still meaningful: the average exchange rate moved from about S$2.01 per U.S. dollar in 1988 to about S$1.31 in 2025. A U.S. dollar became cheaper in Singapore-dollar terms.

Source: SingStat, [Exchange Rates (Average For Period), Annual](https://data.gov.sg/datasets/d_b09aeaf8eb591c4bfe347b66148c6b53/view), checked in May 2026.

This does not mean Singapore is cheap. Chapter 6 already said low tax does not make Singapore cheap. A strong currency also does not make Singapore cheap. In fact, it often travels with the opposite problem. A rich, trusted, high-cost city with a strong currency can feel expensive precisely because everyone is standing on the same strong floor.

But the floor matters.

## The Accusation That Explains the System

In 2019, the U.S. Treasury put Singapore on its monitoring list for currency practices. That sounds dramatic until one remembers what Singapore's monetary policy is.

Singapore manages its currency.

That is not a conspiracy theory. It is the operating manual.

Most central banks talk about interest rates. The Federal Reserve moves the federal funds rate. The Bank of England moves Bank Rate. The European Central Bank moves its policy rates. Singapore is different because Singapore is small, open, and import-dependent. Goods, services, labour, capital, food, energy, and intermediate inputs move through the economy with unusual speed. In that kind of economy, the exchange rate reaches domestic prices more directly than a domestic interest-rate target would.

So MAS runs an exchange-rate-centred monetary policy. The policy variable is the Singapore dollar nominal effective exchange rate, or S$NEER. In plain English, MAS looks not at one bilateral rate like USD/SGD, but at the Singapore dollar against a trade-weighted basket of currencies.

The exact basket is not disclosed. The currency is allowed to move within a policy band. MAS can adjust the path of that band and intervene in foreign-exchange markets when needed to keep the currency aligned with its price-stability objective.

The jargon is intimidating. The household version is simple:

Singapore does not promise one fixed exchange rate. It manages the path of the Singapore dollar against the currencies that matter to Singapore's economy.

When inflation pressure is high, a stronger currency helps. Imports become less expensive than they would otherwise be. Energy, food, equipment, and overseas services do not become free, but the currency absorbs part of the shock. When growth is weak and inflation pressure is lower, MAS can reduce the pace of appreciation. The tool is not a magic wand. It is a steering wheel.

This is why "currency manipulation" is both an accusation and a description with the emotional content removed. Singapore's monetary policy is built around managing the currency. MAS' answer is that the purpose is medium-term price stability, not export advantage. A deliberate weakening of the Singapore dollar would make imported inflation worse, which is a strange strategy for a country that imports so much of what it consumes.

For FI, the important point is not the diplomatic language. It is that Singapore's currency strength is policy-supported. It is not only a market accident.

## Why the Dollar Has Had Upward Pressure

A strong currency does not appear because a country prints a confident-looking banknote.

It needs pressure underneath it.

The old essay behind this chapter made a useful point: for long periods, Singapore's problem has often been not how to stop the currency from collapsing, but how to stop it from strengthening too much. That is why MAS accumulates official foreign reserves. When appreciation pressure on the Singapore dollar is too strong, MAS can buy foreign currency and sell Singapore dollars. That adds to official foreign reserves and releases Singapore-dollar liquidity into the system.

The scale is not small. SingStat's MAS-sourced table of official foreign reserves shows total official foreign reserves of about S$544 billion in April 2026, up from about S$384 billion in January 2023 and about S$289 billion at the end of 2010. These numbers move with valuation changes, market conditions, intervention, and reporting details. They are not a household savings account. But they show the size of the balance sheet behind the currency framework.

Source: SingStat Table Builder, [Official Foreign Reserves (End Of Period), Monthly](https://tablebuilder.singstat.gov.sg/table/TS/M700031), data last updated 7 May 2026.

Where did the upward pressure come from?

Part of the answer is old-fashioned national saving. Singapore's system has many drains that pull Singapore dollars out of immediate household and corporate circulation. CPF contributions are the most familiar one. A worker's salary does not all become spendable cash. Part becomes employee CPF. The employer also contributes. The money remains household wealth, but it is not loose cash chasing goods in the present.

Government borrowing and fiscal rules also keep parts of the public balance sheet from becoming current spending. Some securities exist to build markets, meet CPF needs, provide savings instruments, or transfer excess official foreign reserves. MOF states plainly that the proceeds from many of these borrowings are invested and cannot be spent for recurrent needs.

Fiscal discipline matters too. When a government persistently spends less than it collects, or when land-sale proceeds and certain reserves flows are protected rather than treated as current pocket money, domestic liquidity is restrained. The old essay described this as a supply-side drain. That phrase is slightly technical, but the intuition is right: the Singapore system has built-in mechanisms that prevent every dollar generated in the economy from immediately becoming spendable demand.

That was especially important in earlier decades.

MOF's current explanation adds a second point. In the last decade, it says large capital inflows have chiefly contributed to official foreign reserve accumulation. This is the demand side. Companies invest in Singapore. Funds book activity in Singapore. Wealth moves to Singapore. Family offices, regional headquarters, commodity firms, technology companies, banks, insurers, and asset managers do not all have the same reason to be here, but many of them need Singapore-dollar exposure, Singapore banking rails, Singapore staff, Singapore leases, and Singapore regulatory credibility.

This is less romantic than national mythology and more useful for FI planning. A strong currency is not a medal. It is a price formed by policy, savings, credibility, and demand for the jurisdiction.

## The Reserves Are Not a Piggy Bank

The phrase "Singapore's reserves" causes endless confusion because people use one word for several things.

MAS manages official foreign reserves. Temasek publishes its net portfolio value. GIC manages government assets but does not disclose the full size of the funds it manages.

The Government has assets and liabilities. Fifth Schedule entities have protected reserves. Past Reserves are constitutionally protected. The Net Investment Returns Contribution then allows part of the expected long-term real returns on relevant assets, and part of the net investment income from other past reserves, to be used in the Budget.

This is a complicated machine. The household should not pretend it understands every gear after reading one chapter. It only needs the correct mental model.

The reserves are not money sitting in a drawer waiting for a popular policy idea. They are a national balance sheet. They support confidence. They help MAS conduct monetary policy and maintain financial stability. They provide crisis capacity. They generate investment returns, part of which can support the Budget through NIRC. MOF says NIRC contributes about 20 percent of government revenue annually.

That last point matters for this book. Chapter 6 showed how Singapore can run relatively low taxes while still delivering useful public services. Part of the answer is that the state has investment income. The reserves do not only sit behind the currency. They also help the fiscal system avoid choosing, too brutally, between high taxes and weak services.

But the reserves are not infinite, and the full size is not disclosed. That secrecy is not satisfying to spreadsheet people. Spreadsheet people like cells filled in. The reason given is strategic: a small country with no natural resources does not want to publish the full size of its war chest. Whether one finds that emotionally satisfying is separate from the FI point.

The FI point is this: a Singapore household is living inside a country with an unusually strong public balance sheet. That balance sheet does not belong to the household. You cannot withdraw it at an ATM. But it affects the currency, the fiscal system, the crisis response, the credit rating, and the confidence other people have when deciding whether to hold Singapore-dollar exposure.

You do not own the national balance sheet.

You stand on it.

## What the Strong Dollar Buys

For a household pursuing FI, a strong Singapore dollar buys four kinds of optionality.

First, it protects imported purchasing power. Singapore imports a great deal of what it consumes. Food, energy, medicine, electronics, vehicles, building materials, travel, and overseas services all pass through foreign prices. A stronger currency does not stop global inflation. It changes how much of global inflation lands in Singapore-dollar terms.

Second, it clarifies the jobs inside the portfolio. Global equities diversify the productive engine. SGD cash, CPF, local bonds, and local-income assets match part of the spending currency. A Singaporean buying a global equity ETF may be converting SGD into USD or another fund currency, and currency movements will still affect reported returns. That is not a reason to avoid global equities. It is a reason to know which assets are meant to grow the portfolio and which assets are meant to pay Singapore-dollar bills. These are different jobs.

Third, it powers geographic arbitrage. A Singapore dollar spent in Singapore buys a Singapore life. A Singapore dollar spent in Malaysia, Thailand, Vietnam, Indonesia, or parts of southern Europe may buy a different life. This does not mean everyone should move. Elderly parents, children, school systems, visas, healthcare, language, community, and personal temperament are real constraints. But optionality has value even when unused. A strong currency gives a household more ways to solve the same spending problem.

Fourth, it changes how emergencies feel. A family with SGD assets, access to global markets, and a credible home-country currency has more room to respond to overseas education, medical choices, temporary relocation, family support abroad, or a career break outside Singapore. Again, not free. Not frictionless. But easier.

This is why currency belongs in the FI stack. The same portfolio size can support different lives depending on where the spending happens and what currency the spending is in.

S$1 million is not S$1 million in every geography.

## The Price of the Floor

Strong currency talk can become smug very quickly.

That is dangerous because currencies do not rise in a morality play. A strong Singapore dollar has benefits, but it also has costs and trade-offs.

For exporters and firms competing on price, a stronger currency can hurt. Revenue earned abroad may translate into fewer Singapore dollars. Costs paid in Singapore dollars may remain high. Tourism can become more expensive for visitors. Local wages and rents can become harder to justify for some lower-margin activities. If the currency does too much of the anti-inflation work, domestic interest rates and asset-market conditions still follow global forces in messy ways. Singapore does not get to control every variable just because it controls one important variable.

For households, the strong floor can also hide the cost level. A Singaporean may feel rich in Johor Bahru and squeezed at home in the same weekend. That is not hypocrisy. It is currency plus local scarcity. Land is scarce. Labour is expensive. Cars are policy-expensive. Private housing is expensive. Many services are expensive because high-income people bid for other people's time.

So the right conclusion is not:

`Singapore has a strong dollar, therefore Singapore life is cheap.`

The right conclusion is:

`Singapore has a strong dollar, therefore Singapore income and assets can have unusually useful external purchasing power.`

Those are different sentences.

The first one is false. The second one is the FI lever.

## If the Dollar Stops Being Strong

Every FI plan contains an implied macro bet. Most people simply do not write it down.

If you keep all assets in SGD and spend only in Singapore, you are betting on Singapore's currency, institutions, inflation control, property system, healthcare system, and political order. If you keep almost all assets in U.S. equities, you are betting on U.S. corporate capitalism, U.S. market structure, and the U.S. dollar. If you plan to retire in Malaysia on Singapore assets, you are betting on both Singapore's relative currency strength and Malaysia remaining accessible, liveable, and politically workable for you.

There is no version of FI with no macro bet. There are only bets you can see and bets you forgot you made.

The strong-SGD thesis depends on assumptions. Singapore must remain institutionally credible. MAS must retain the ability and willingness to manage the currency for price stability. The public balance sheet must remain strong. The economy must continue to attract capital and talent. Social cohesion must remain good enough that the system can keep making long-term decisions. Regional alternatives must remain accessible if geographic arbitrage is part of the plan.

None of these is guaranteed for forty years.

The old essay ended with a useful discomfort: believing the Singapore dollar will remain strong forever is, at some level, long-term optimism about Singapore's national trajectory. It may be reasonable optimism. It may even be the best available base case for someone whose life, family, property, CPF, and career are already here. But it is still a bet.

For FI planning, the answer is not to panic. The answer is to avoid single-point failure.

Do not build a plan that only works if SGD keeps appreciating against every currency you may ever spend. Do not build a plan that only works if U.S. assets keep winning and SGD weakness never matters. Do not build a plan that only works if Malaysia stays cheap, friendly, and administratively easy. Do not build a plan that only works if Singapore property remains liquid at the exact moment you need to downgrade.

Robustness is less glamorous than conviction. It is also more useful.

## The Practical Rule

Match currencies by job.

Keep near-term Singapore spending in Singapore dollars. This includes emergency cash, near-term housing costs, school fees, insurance deductibles, and the part of the portfolio meant to protect against being forced to sell volatile assets at the wrong time.

Treat CPF as a long-term SGD asset with policy risk and liquidity gates. Chapter 5 already covered the caveats. In this chapter, the currency point is simple: CPF is not only a retirement system. It is also a large SGD-denominated balance-sheet component for many Singapore households.

Own global productive assets for long-term growth. A Singaporean FI portfolio should not become a patriotic pile of local assets simply because the Singapore dollar has been strong. The local stock market is small and concentrated. The world economy is larger than Singapore. Global equities, held through sensible low-cost funds, solve a different problem from SGD cash and CPF.

Be honest about future spending geography. If the plan is to spend most of life in Singapore, do not pretend a Johor Bahru budget solves the whole problem. If the plan includes part-time or full-time regional living, then the strong Singapore dollar is a real asset, but it comes with visa, healthcare, family, and lifestyle constraints. Write those constraints down before calling the plan complete.

In my own planning, I would not treat a cheaper country as the plan. I would treat it as an option. The plan still has to work with Singapore liabilities, Singapore family obligations, and Singapore medical reality. Regional optionality is the bonus door, not the foundation.

Finally, include a currency stress test. Ask what happens if SGD weakens materially for a decade, or if the country where you hoped to spend becomes less cheap, less welcoming, or less safe. The goal is not to forecast disaster. The goal is to prevent a single macro story from carrying the whole retirement.

Singapore's strong dollar is a feature. It is not a promise.

Used well, it expands the household's map. It makes overseas options more real, imported shocks less brutal, and SGD-denominated safety assets more meaningful. Used badly, it becomes another national slogan, the macroeconomic version of buying a condo because the brochure says "exclusive".

FI does not require slogans. It requires mechanisms.

CPF is a mechanism. Low tax is a mechanism. A strong, credible currency is a mechanism. The next mechanism is the largest expense in most lives, and the one most likely to decide whether the Singapore FI stack remains a shortcut or becomes a trap.

We need to talk about housing.
