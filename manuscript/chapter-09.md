# Why We Left the Condo

We sold our condo and bought a five-room HDB flat.

That sentence sounds like a confession in some Singapore circles. It can be heard as moving backward, giving up private facilities, leaving the property ladder, or admitting that the original private-property decision was a mistake.

It was not that dramatic.

The first home I bought in Singapore was an OCR, or outside central region, condo unit. We lived there for almost four years. It was a real home. The estate had facilities, security, landscaped common areas, a private-property address, and the quiet middle-class satisfaction of having bought the respectable thing. Later we sold it and bought a five-room HDB flat in a more convenient location.

At the time, the decision was mostly about life. Commute. Space. Children. Daily rhythm. The HDB flat made the week easier.

Only later did I understand the FI consequence. We had not merely changed homes. We had changed the cost structure of the household.

A condo can be a good home. The mistake is calling every good home an FI shortcut.

Chapter 8 argued that HDB can lower the floor. This chapter is about the escalator.

## What the Condo Sells

A condo is not one product.

It is a home, an investment story, a facilities subscription, a status signal, a maintenance bill, a leveraged bet on Singapore land policy, and a committee membership with hundreds of strangers.

Most buyers price the first two. They ask whether the family likes the unit and whether the price will rise. Those are important questions. They are not enough.

The home part is real. Some households genuinely value the privacy, layout, facilities, location, school commute, grandparents nearby, or the simple pleasure of living in a place they like. A home is not a spreadsheet cell with a roof.

The investment story is also not invented from nothing. Singapore private residential prices have risen over long periods.

URA's private residential property price index for non-landed homes goes back to 1975. On the data.gov.sg series, the non-landed index was 10.5 in 1975-Q1 and 210.8 in 2026-Q1, about a twentyfold increase. That is roughly 6 percent a year in price-index terms. Starting the clock in 1983, after the earliest and noisier period, the same non-landed index rose from 40.6 to 210.8 by 2026-Q1, roughly 3.9 percent a year.[1](#note-1)

That is a respectable record. It is also not the whole return of an owner-occupied condo, and it is not a retirement plan by itself.

The price index is not your household cash flow. It does not subtract mortgage interest, maintenance fees, property tax, renovation, stamp duty, agent fees, legal fees, insurance, repairs, vacancy if rented out later, or the opportunity cost of the down payment. It also does not solve the Chapter 4 problem: while you live in the condo, you cannot sell 3.5 percent of the master bedroom each year to pay for groceries.

Home equity is wealth. Owner-occupied home equity is not automatic FI income.

That distinction is easy to forget because property stories are told in sale prices. "We bought at S$1.2 million and sold at S$1.8 million" is a clean dinner-table sentence. The household version is messier: how much cash went in, how much CPF went in, how much interest was paid, how much maintenance was paid, what taxes and fees applied, what alternative assets were not bought, and what annual spending floor the home created while the family lived there.

The condo can still be worth it. But the bundle has to be priced honestly.

## Compared With What?

Singapore property discussions often pretend the comparison is between buying a condo and doing nothing.

That is the wrong comparison. Money always goes somewhere.

A down payment can become home equity. It can also become a global equity portfolio, a paid-off HDB loan, a liquid bridge, a CPF top-up, a business buffer, or simply the absence of debt. A monthly maintenance fee can buy facilities. It can also buy index-fund units. Mortgage interest can buy leverage. It can also buy risk.

This is why the old "property always goes up" line is not enough. Even if the price goes up, the FI question remains: compared with what, after costs, and with what effect on the household's freedom?

The comparison does not need to prove that stocks are always better than property. They are different assets. A global equity fund does not house your children. A condo does not give you daily liquidity. Property is lumpy, leveraged, location-specific, policy-sensitive, and expensive to transact. Equities are volatile, liquid, globally diversified if bought properly, and emotionally hard to hold during crashes.

The useful comparison is not ideological. It is functional.

What job is this dollar doing?

If the dollar buys a home the family loves and can comfortably afford, fine. If it buys a leveraged belief that private property will save a weak savings rate, the household is asking the wrong asset to do the wrong job.

Singapore also makes the property story less wild than many people imagine. The state has a large policy toolbox. Demand is shaped by citizenship, PR approvals, ABSD, TDSR, LTV limits, and other cooling measures. Supply is shaped through land sales, planning, redevelopment rules, and public housing policy. URA's 1Q2026 release is typical of the official tone: prices rose, supply was coming, and households were reminded to exercise prudence when buying property and taking mortgage loans.[2](#note-2)

This is not a market where middle-class buyers should assume quick riches will go unanswered by policy.

The realistic private-property thesis is more modest: private property may grow with Singapore, sometimes strongly, sometimes weakly, sometimes after long flat periods. That can be a reasonable life decision. It is not a cheat code.

## The Non-Cancellable Gym Membership

Facilities are not fake.

A pool is useful if the family swims. A gym is useful if someone actually trains. A function room is useful if the family hosts. Security may matter to some households. A landscaped estate may make daily life feel calmer. None of this should be dismissed by someone whose own preferences happen to be cheaper.

The problem is utilisation.

A condo facilities bill often behaves like a gym membership attached to a mortgage. The household pays for the possibility of use, not the use itself. The difference is that a gym membership can usually be cancelled. The condo version can only be cancelled by selling the home.

In a private strata development, the management corporation collects contributions for maintenance, repair, renewal, insurance, common property, and estate management. The monthly bill is not a decorative charge. It is the financial metabolism of the building.[3](#note-3)

That means the bill should be evaluated like any other recurring cost. At a 3.5 percent withdrawal rate, every S$500 a month of permanent cost needs about S$171,000 of additional FI asset base.

`S$6,000 / 0.035 = about S$171,000`

At S$800 a month, the FI target moves by about S$274,000.

The question is not whether condo facilities are good or bad. The question is whether this household uses them enough to justify the permanent rise in the floor.

If the children swim three times a week, the adults use the gym, grandparents enjoy the sheltered estate, and the function room replaces other paid venues, the facilities are buying real life. If the pool is a blue rectangle viewed from the lift lobby, the gym is a moral aspiration with dumbbells, and the security guard mainly waves at delivery riders, the household has bought a story with a monthly invoice.

Stories are allowed. FI only asks that they be priced.

## The Private-Property Carry

A condo also comes with carrying costs that do not appear in the headline price.

Start with debt. A private-property buyer typically relies on a bank loan, subject to regulated borrowing limits. The current public CPF home-buying guide explains the basic shape for members below 55: TDSR should be less than or equal to 55 percent of gross monthly income, and LTV can be up to 75 percent under the stated conditions.[4](#note-4)

Those limits are guardrails for lenders and the system. They are not an FI affordability target.

A household can pass TDSR and still buy a home that keeps it dependent on peak salary for too long. The bank asks whether the household can service the debt. FI asks whether the household still has enough surplus to become free.

Then come transaction taxes and ownership taxes. Buyer's Stamp Duty applies to property purchases. ABSD can apply depending on buyer profile and property count; a Singapore Citizen buying a second residential property faces a very different stamp-duty problem from a Singapore Citizen buying a first residential property.[5](#note-5) Residential property tax is also progressive, with owner-occupier and non-owner-occupier schedules.[6](#note-6)

None of these costs means "do not buy." They mean "do not round the cost to the purchase price."

The household also has renovation, repairs, insurance, mortgage repricing risk, legal fees, agent fees on sale, and the CPF refund mechanics described in Chapter 8. CPF used for housing is not gone, but it has moved into the wall. If the home later sells, CPF principal used and accrued interest generally have to be refunded to the CPF account, subject to CPF rules.

All of this creates a private-property carry. The asset may rise. The carry must be paid while the household waits.

For FI, waiting is not free. Waiting is years of employment income required to support the floor.

## Lease, Strata, And the En Bloc Lottery

Many Singapore condos are 99-year leasehold properties. Some are freehold or 999-year leasehold. Freehold is a better word to see on a listing, but it is not magic.

High-rise buildings age. Waterproofing fails. Lifts age. Facades need repair. Pipes and electrical systems become less charming over time. Common facilities that looked luxurious at launch can become expensive to keep respectable. The older the estate, the more maintenance becomes a governance problem as well as an engineering problem.

That is where strata ownership matters.

In an HDB estate, the state sits behind a large part of the physical system. In a condo, the owners collectively own and govern the common property through the strata structure. That collective ownership can work well. It can also be slow, political, underfunded, or dominated by people whose interests do not match yours.

When you own one unit, you own one vote-sized slice of a shared machine. The value of your unit depends partly on decisions you do not control: how much to maintain, when to repair, whether to upgrade, how to enforce by-laws, whether enough owners agree on major decisions, and eventually whether an en bloc sale is possible.

En bloc is the property-market version of a lottery ticket with minutes, lawyers, and years of meetings.

It can create real wealth for some owners. It can also fail after repeated attempts. It depends on timing, plot ratio, developer appetite, construction costs, interest rates, lease age, owners' expectations, and whether enough people agree. It should be treated as optional upside, not as the plan that makes the purchase affordable.

If a condo purchase only works because of a future en bloc, it does not work yet.

## The FI Arithmetic

Now return to the formula.

Chapter 4 uses:

`FI asset base x 0.035 / annual spending`

A condo can hurt both sides at once.

First, it can reduce the FI asset base. A large down payment, buyer's stamp duty, renovation, and ongoing cash costs move money away from liquid or investable assets. Some of that becomes home equity, which belongs in net worth. But unless the plan includes selling, downsizing, renting out, or moving away, it does not sit inside the 3.5 percent withdrawal base.

Second, it can raise annual spending. Mortgage interest, maintenance fees, property tax, insurance, repairs, and the lifestyle standard around the home increase the floor that the portfolio must later support.

That is the dangerous combination: less liquid portfolio, higher spending floor.

Imagine a household with S$500,000 that could stay in liquid investments, CPF balances, or a smaller home with a smaller loan. It could keep a modest home and let much of that remain part of the FI engine. Or it could use the S$500,000 as the down payment and transaction-cost base for a much larger private-property purchase. The household may be richer on paper if the condo appreciates. It may also need many more years of salary to build a liquid bridge outside the home.

This is why "we can afford the monthly instalment" is too low a bar.

Affordability is not freedom. Affordability only says the household can carry the obligation today. FI asks whether carrying it delays the day when employment income becomes optional.

The condo can still pass that test. A high-income household with strong liquid assets, stable income, clear family reasons, low other fixed costs, and no need to pretend the condo is a retirement product may buy private property without damaging the plan. A household that loves the condo, uses the facilities, prices the carry, and still reaches FI on schedule is not making a mistake.

The problem is buying the escalator and calling it an accelerator.

## The Right Reason to Buy

There are good reasons to buy a condo.

Privacy can matter. Facilities can matter. A specific location can matter. A shorter commute can return hundreds of hours. A family may need to live near parents, school, work, or medical care. Some households want the private-estate environment and can comfortably pay for it. Some dislike the restrictions attached to HDB. Some are not eligible for HDB in the first place.

Those are real reasons.

The book is not here to turn every household into the cheapest possible version of itself. The cheapest home that damages the marriage, breaks the commute, isolates grandparents, or makes daily life miserable is not cheap. It is deferred cost.

But a life-quality decision should be named as a life-quality decision.

If the condo is consumption, price it as consumption. If it is an investment, compare it with other investments after costs. If it is both, separate the two jobs. The home can make life better while the investment case remains mediocre. The investment case can be acceptable while the home makes the household cash-poor. Mixing the two is how expensive decisions become emotionally protected from arithmetic.

The most dangerous sentence in Singapore housing is not "I want a nicer home."

It is "property is different."

Property is different in many ways. It is usable, leveraged, local, emotional, tax-shaped, policy-shaped, and hard to sell in pieces. Those differences make it more important to analyse, not less.

## The Practical Rule

Before buying a condo, run four tests.

First, remove the resale-profit assumption. If the price merely keeps pace with inflation for a decade, does the household plan still work?

Second, remove the en bloc dream. If the estate remains an ageing strata property rather than turning into a lottery ticket, does the decision still make sense?

Third, remove permanent peak salary. If one income falls, bonuses shrink, or the industry reprices the household, can the mortgage and maintenance still be carried without selling at the wrong time?

Fourth, remove the identity premium. If nobody were impressed by the address, would the family still buy this specific home for this specific life?

If the answer is still yes, the condo may be a good home the household can afford. Enjoy it honestly.

If the answer becomes no, the condo is doing too much narrative work. It is not a cheat code. It is a higher floor with a better lobby.

The practical rule is simple: price the bundle honestly.

Do not buy facilities you will not use, status you cannot retire on, leverage you cannot carry, or an investment story that requires policy, interest rates, buyers, and neighbours to cooperate for twenty years.

Chapter 8 showed how HDB can lower the floor. This chapter showed how private housing can raise it. The next part of the book moves from the home to daily life, where the same pattern repeats in smaller bills: food, geography, children, and the skills that keep convenience from becoming a permanent tax.
