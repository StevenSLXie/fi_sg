# Why We Left the Condo

We sold our condo and bought a five-room HDB flat.

In some Singapore circles that sounds like moving backward. Private to public. Facilities to void deck. Property ladder to something less shiny.

That is the story the market wants you to hear. It is also why the story needs to be priced.

The first home I bought in Singapore was an OCR, or outside central region, condo unit. We lived there for almost four years. It was a real home. The estate had facilities, security, landscaped common areas, and the quiet middle-class satisfaction of having bought the respectable thing. Later we sold it and bought a five-room HDB flat in a more convenient location.

At the time, the move was mostly about daily life. Commute. Space. Children. Rhythm.

Only later did I understand the FI consequence. We had not merely changed homes. We had changed the cost structure of the household.

A condo can be a good home. The mistake is calling every good home an FI shortcut.

Chapter 8 showed how HDB can lower the floor. A condo often does the opposite. It can be a home, an investment story, a facilities subscription, a status signal, a maintenance bill, a leveraged bet on Singapore land policy, and a committee membership with hundreds of strangers. The buyer usually prices the home and the investment story. The FI damage often hides in the rest.

Start with the lazy sales pitch that private property automatically compounds better than HDB. Then make the self-occupied condo problem impossible to miss: for most middle-class households, a large amount of capital gets locked inside the home, produces no spending cash while they live in it, and raises the annual cost that the FI portfolio must support.

That is a bad shape for early FI.

## The Appreciation Story Is Weaker Than Advertised

The standard condo story begins with appreciation.

Private property, we are told, is the real asset. HDB is for living. Condo is for wealth. If you can stretch to buy private, you should. The private market will reward you.

Maybe. But the official indices do not support the lazy version of that claim.

Use the longest common window where the HDB resale price index and the URA non-landed private residential price index can be compared from the same start date. From 1990-Q1 to 2026-Q1, the HDB resale price index rose from 24.3 to 203.4. That is about 8.37 times, or roughly 6.08 percent a year. Over the same period, the URA non-landed private residential price index rose from 41.6 to 210.8. That is about 5.07 times, or roughly 4.61 percent a year.[1](#note-1)

On the longest official window where the two series can be compared from the same start date, HDB resale did not merely keep up with non-landed private housing. It beat it.

That does not prove HDB will always win. It does prove that the sales pitch "private property automatically compounds better" is too lazy for an FI plan.

There are caveats, and they matter. These are price indices, not total household returns. They exclude rent, leverage, mortgage interest, taxes, stamp duties, maintenance, renovation, transaction costs, CPF usage, grants, eligibility restrictions, and the difference between one flat in one town and one condo in one district. The HDB series tracks resale flats. The URA series tracks non-landed private residential properties. They are not identical products.

Not every window favours HDB either. From 2009-Q1 to 2026-Q1, both series use 100 as the base. Over that period, non-landed private rose to 210.8, while HDB resale rose to 203.4. Private was slightly ahead.

Good. That is the point. There is no automatic law.

Private property may outperform in some periods. HDB resale may outperform in others. Individual projects can do much better or much worse than the index. But once the slogan becomes "private always wins", the household has stopped analysing and started repeating marketing.

For FI, the important question is not whether a condo can appreciate. Of course it can. The important question is whether the appreciation, after costs and while you are living inside the asset, helps you become work-optional faster than the alternatives.

That is a much harder question.

## The Capital Is Trapped While You Live In It

Here is the sharper problem.

A self-occupied condo can make you wealthier on paper while pushing FI further away.

That sounds contradictory only if net worth and FI cash flow are treated as the same thing. They are not. Chapter 4 separated the broad balance sheet from the FI asset base. The home you live in belongs on the balance sheet. It does not automatically belong in the 3.5 percent withdrawal base unless the plan includes selling it, downsizing it, renting it out, moving away, or otherwise turning that equity into spending power.

For a middle-class household, the condo is often the largest capital allocation of its life. The down payment goes in. CPF goes in. Years of principal repayment go in. Renovation goes in. Transaction costs go in. If the property rises, the balance sheet looks better.

But while the family is living there, that capital does not buy groceries, pay school fees, fund a career break, or replace salary.

At the same time, the condo raises the cost floor. Mortgage interest, maintenance contributions, property tax, insurance, repairs, and the lifestyle standard around the home all have to be paid in real cash or CPF flow. The household now needs a larger portfolio to support a more expensive life.

This is the double hit.

The FI asset base shrinks because liquid capital has moved into owner-occupied housing. Annual spending rises because the home is more expensive to carry.

That is the opposite of what an FI accelerator should do.

At a 3.5 percent withdrawal rate, every additional S$10,000 of annual recurring cost requires about S$286,000 more FI asset base.

`S$10,000 / 0.035 = about S$286,000`

S$20,000 a year requires about S$571,000 more.

This is why "we can afford the monthly instalment" is a weak test. The bank asks whether the household can service the debt. FI asks whether the household can become free while servicing it.

Those are different exams.

Imagine a household with S$500,000 that could remain in liquid investments, CPF balances, or a smaller home with a smaller loan. If that S$500,000 becomes the down payment and transaction-cost base for a larger private-property purchase, the household may still be solvent. It may even look richer later. But it now needs to rebuild the liquid bridge outside the home while carrying a higher cost base.

For early FI, that is not a small inconvenience. It is the core conflict.

If the household does not plan to sell, downgrade, rent out, or leave Singapore, the condo's paper gain is not funding the FI lifestyle. It is a number trapped in the wall.

## What the Condo Bundle Adds

The rest of the condo bundle matters because it supports that same problem.

Facilities are the cleanest example. A pool is useful if the family swims. A gym is useful if someone actually trains. A function room is useful if the family hosts. Security and landscaping may improve daily life. A condo can absolutely buy real quality of life.

But the bill behaves like a facilities subscription attached to a mortgage. You cannot cancel it when the children stop swimming or the adults stop using the gym. In a private strata development, the management corporation collects contributions for maintenance, repair, renewal, insurance, common property, and estate management.[2](#note-2)

The question is not whether facilities are good or bad. The question is whether the household uses them enough to justify the permanent rise in the floor.

If the household pays S$500 a month in additional recurring cost, the FI target moves by about S$171,000.

`S$6,000 / 0.035 = about S$171,000`

If the facilities are used constantly, that may be a life-quality choice worth paying for. If the pool is scenery, the gym is aspiration, and the security guard mainly waves at delivery riders, the household has bought a story with a monthly invoice.

Strata ownership adds another layer. In an HDB estate, the state sits behind a large part of the physical system. In a condo, owners collectively govern the common property. That can work well. It can also become slow, political, underfunded, or dominated by neighbours whose incentives do not match yours.

Your unit value depends partly on decisions you do not control: maintenance, repairs, by-laws, reserve funds, upgrades, disputes, and eventually whether an en bloc sale is possible.

En bloc can create real wealth. It is also a lottery ticket with meetings, lawyers, timing risk, developer appetite, construction costs, interest rates, lease age, plot ratio, and owners who may not agree. It should be treated as optional upside, not as the plan that makes a purchase affordable.

If a condo only works because of future en bloc, it does not work yet.

## The Borrowing Limit Is Not The FI Limit

A private-property buyer also enters a more expensive financing structure.

A buyer may pass the regulated borrowing tests. For members below 55, CPF's public home-buying guide describes TDSR as total monthly debt obligations including the new loan being less than or equal to 55 percent of gross monthly income, and gives examples where LTV can be up to 75 percent under stated conditions.[3](#note-3)

Those are system guardrails. They are not a household freedom target.

A household can pass TDSR and still buy a home that keeps it dependent on peak salary for too long. It can pass LTV and still convert too much future income into a housing obligation. It can pass the bank's test and fail the FI test.

Then come transaction and ownership costs. Buyer's Stamp Duty applies to property purchases. ABSD can apply depending on buyer profile and property count.[4](#note-4) Residential property tax is progressive, with different owner-occupier and non-owner-occupier schedules.[5](#note-5) Renovation, repairs, mortgage repricing risk, legal fees, agent fees, insurance, and CPF refund mechanics do not disappear because the unit has a nice lobby.

None of this means "never buy a condo."

It means the purchase price is the beginning of the cost, not the cost.

## The Right Reason To Buy

There are good reasons to buy a condo.

Privacy can matter. Facilities can matter. A specific location can matter. A shorter commute can return hundreds of hours. A family may need to live near parents, school, work, or medical care. Some households are not eligible for HDB. Some dislike HDB restrictions. Some simply want the private-estate environment and can comfortably afford it.

Those are real reasons.

The book is not trying to turn every household into the cheapest possible version of itself. The cheapest home that damages the marriage, breaks the commute, isolates grandparents, or makes daily life miserable is not cheap. It is deferred cost.

But a life-quality decision should be named as a life-quality decision.

If the condo is consumption, price it as consumption. If it is an investment, compare it with other investments after costs. If it is both, separate the two jobs. The home can make life better while the investment case remains mediocre. The investment case can look good while the home makes the household cash-poor.

Mixing the two is how expensive decisions become emotionally protected from arithmetic.

The most dangerous sentence in Singapore housing is not "I want a nicer home."

It is "property is different."

Property is different. It is usable, leveraged, local, emotional, tax-shaped, policy-shaped, and hard to sell in pieces. Those differences make it more important to analyse, not less.

## The Practical Rule

Before buying a condo, run four tests.

First, remove the appreciation slogan. If private property does not automatically beat HDB, does the purchase still make sense?

Second, remove the cash-flow illusion. If the home equity cannot fund spending while you live there, does the household still have enough liquid FI asset base outside the property?

Third, remove permanent peak salary. If one income falls, bonuses shrink, mortgage rates move, or the industry reprices the household, can the mortgage and maintenance still be carried without selling at the wrong time?

Fourth, remove the identity premium. If nobody were impressed by the address, would the family still buy this specific home for this specific life?

If the answer is still yes, the condo may be a good home the household can afford. Enjoy it honestly.

If the answer becomes no, the condo is doing too much narrative work.

For early FI, the default burden of proof should be on the condo, not on the cheaper home. The condo has to explain why locking more capital into a self-occupied asset, raising the cost floor, and adding recurring obligations makes work optional sooner.

Sometimes it can. Usually it cannot.

The practical rule is simple: do not buy a higher floor and call it a shortcut.

Chapter 8 showed how HDB can lower the floor. This chapter showed why private housing often raises it. The next part of the book moves from the home to daily life, where the same pattern repeats in smaller bills: food, geography, children, and the skills that keep convenience from becoming a permanent tax.
