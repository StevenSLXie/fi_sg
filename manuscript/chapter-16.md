# What Can Go Wrong?

> **Navigation:** [← How Much Is Enough?](chapter-15.md) · [Contents](../SUMMARY.md) · Next: forthcoming

The spreadsheet reaches 100 percent.

Then the first bad year arrives.

Markets fall. The headlines become historical. People who sounded bored last year now sound certain that the old rules are over. Rent, school fees, insurance premiums, and supermarket bills continue to arrive with no interest in your asset allocation.

This is the part of FI that no calculator can remove.

Financial independence begins with assumptions: returns, spending, inflation, CPF timing, housing, health, family. Some of them will be wrong. The useful question is what can still move when they do.

A good FI plan does not survive because nothing bad happens.

It survives because several things can still move.

## The First Bad Year

The obvious risk is a market crash.

Everyone thinks they understand this one. Stocks fall. The portfolio goes down. The household feels poorer. If the person is still working, the answer is unpleasant but familiar: keep earning, keep buying, wait.

After FI, the same crash feels different.

Now the portfolio is no longer just a score. It is the grocery bill, the school bill, the mortgage bridge, and the reason you told yourself work was optional. A 35 percent fall during accumulation is a sale. A 35 percent fall in the first year after quitting is a test of whether the plan was real.

That is sequence risk. The average return over thirty years may be fine, but the order matters. A poor early sequence can do much more damage than the same bad returns arriving after the portfolio has had years to compound. This is why withdrawal-rate backtests spend so much time on difficult starting years such as the mid-1960s and 1970s: the bad combination is not merely falling prices, but falling prices plus inflation plus withdrawals.[1](#note-1)

The wrong lesson is to wait until the world looks safe.

It never does. There is always a reason not to buy, not to retire, not to trust the number. If the plan requires a calm market and a calm mind in the same year, it is fragile.

The better lesson is to build the bad year into the plan before it arrives.

## The Buffer Is Built Before The Crash

My own answer is not to predict crashes.

I do not know when the next one will happen. I do not know whether the trigger will be inflation, war, credit, China, America, AI, politics, or something that currently looks harmless. I do not want my family's FI plan to require that forecast.

So the protection has to be structural.

Start with diversification. My own portfolio is unusually index-heavy and spread across global and U.S. equities, with small local and Hong Kong exposure, some precious metals, a little Bitcoin, and cash. Nobody should copy the exact allocation. The philosophy matters more: I do not want one company, one country, one currency, or one macro story to decide the household's future.

I keep a war chest. The phrase sounds more dramatic than the function. I do not hold cash because I can call the bottom. I hold it because a household behaves better when it is not forced to sell the wrong asset at the wrong time. Cash can pay bills, rebalance the portfolio, and fund the automatic plan when the news says run.

Then there is the withdrawal rate itself. Using 3.5 percent instead of 4 percent is margin. The difference looks small on paper. In a bad sequence, small margins can become real air. Moving from 4 percent to 5 percent to 6 percent is not a polite linear change; the historical failure risk can worsen sharply once withdrawals become too aggressive.[1](#note-1)

I do not count the home we live in as part of the FI asset base. That makes the number harder. It also leaves a last-resort asset outside the ordinary withdrawal machine. I do not want to build a plan that depends on selling the home. But in a true emergency, a home is not nothing.

None of these layers makes a crash pleasant.

They make it less likely that the first bad year forces the household to abandon the plan at the worst possible price.

## The Singapore Bet

The less obvious risk is the system.

A Singapore household can easily build an FI plan that is heavily exposed to Singapore without noticing. Salary, CPF, housing, school, and the late-life floor may all depend on the same national system.

That concentration is not foolish. Much of this book argues that Singapore is unusually useful for FI. CPF, HDB, low taxes, public safety, transport, schools, healthcare, and a strong currency are not small advantages.

But they are still one national system.

The uncomfortable question is what happens if that system becomes less exceptional.

Singapore is aging. The citizen share aged 65 and above rose from 13.1 percent in 2015 to 20.7 percent in 2025, and official projections put it near one in four by 2030. The resident total fertility rate was 0.97 in 2024.[2](#note-2) At the same time, Singapore's economic model still relies heavily on foreign labour and capital. The 2025 population report puts the non-resident population at 1.91 million, with the foreign workforce making up about two-thirds of that non-resident population.[2](#note-2)

Those facts do not predict collapse.

They explain why politics, labour, immigration, fiscal capacity, and social trust belong on a personal balance sheet. A country can be brilliantly run for decades and still face harder trade-offs later. If Singapore's institutional quality weakens, CPF confidence, SGD purchasing power, home values, and rental demand may weaken together.

This is why I am cautious about turning every CPF optimisation into a one-way bet.

The classic example is moving Ordinary Account money into the Special Account. The arithmetic can look excellent: higher interest, more retirement compounding, stronger future payouts. CPF itself explains that OA is used for housing and other needs, SA is mainly for retirement, and transfers from OA to SA can boost retirement savings.[3](#note-3)

But CPF also says the transfer is irreversible and warns that moving OA savings to SA reduces flexibility for housing or mortgage needs.[3](#note-3)

That matters.

CPF is real wealth. Liquidity is also real wealth. A young household that sells all flexibility for a higher credited rate is concentrating trust in future rules, future liquidity gates, and future Singapore-dollar purchasing power.

The Singapore dollar is strong because the system behind it is strong. MAS explains that official foreign reserves accumulate when it purchases foreign currency and sells Singapore dollars to moderate appreciation pressure. Those pressures have come from public-sector operations that withdraw Singapore-dollar liquidity and from capital inflows reflecting confidence in Singapore.[4](#note-4)

Confidence is an asset. It can also weaken.

A good Singapore FI plan can trust the system without pretending the system is outside risk. Keep some global assets, keep enough liquidity, and keep some decisions reversible. Treat CPF as part of the floor, not the only floor. Remember that a strong currency is a national achievement, not a private entitlement.

## The Family Shock

Some risks do not care about markets or governments.

A serious illness, disability, or death can change the household faster than any index chart. This is where basic insurance belongs.

My own view is deliberately plain. In Singapore, a sensible hospitalisation setup and term life cover are enough for my household's core catastrophe risks. The exact product is not the point. Hospitalisation insurance transfers the risk of a large medical bill. Term life protects dependants if income disappears because a parent dies. Beyond that, insurance can quickly become another way to buy comfort from fear.

The state already provides a healthcare floor through MediShield Life, and Integrated Shield Plans add private-insurer coverage on top for higher ward classes or private hospitals.[5](#note-5) That can be useful. MoneySense also treats insurance as protection against financial loss, not as a collectible hobby.[6](#note-6)

The principle is simple: insure the event that can break the household, then stop. Do not make insurance the centre of the FI story. The centre is still spending, assets, liquidity, and the ability to respond.

## The Moves That Remain

If the plan begins to fail, the household should not discover its options for the first time in panic.

There are only a few big moves.

Cut spending first. This is why fixed costs mattered so much earlier in the book. A household with a low floor can respond to a bad year by lowering discretionary spending. A household with a large mortgage, a car loan, private-school commitments, and a restaurant routine has fewer knobs to turn.

Use cash next. That is what the war chest is for. Not to prove market timing genius. Not to make every dip exciting. To prevent a temporary market decline from becoming a forced liquidation.

Then sell liquid assets in a controlled way. Dividends are not required. A total-return portfolio can create cash by selling units. The important distinction is between selling a planned amount from a diversified portfolio and selling whatever is necessary from a small concentrated position after it has already collapsed.

Keep the ability to work alive.

FI means work is optional. Optional should not mean impossible. A programmer should still be able to program. A finance person should still understand finance. A teacher should still be able to teach, tutor, write, or consult. Even small work income can dramatically reduce withdrawal pressure in a bad sequence.

Skills are not only for earning more before FI. They are also a recovery option after FI.

Keep the geographic valve visible.

I do not want geography arbitrage to take over the book. But it is a real escape valve. A Singapore home, if it can legally be rented out, can produce income that is meaningful in many lower-cost places. HDB rules matter here: Singapore Citizen flat owners generally need to fulfil the Minimum Occupation Period and obtain the relevant approval before renting out the whole flat, and newer flat categories can have stricter restrictions.[7](#note-7) This option is not available to everyone tomorrow.

Still, the option has value.

If a household owns a Singapore flat, can rent it out legally, and is willing to live elsewhere for a period, the same portfolio can last much longer. In many lower-cost places, Singapore rental income plus a modest portfolio can buy more time than the same dollars inside Singapore. The plan does not need to assume this will happen. It should know whether it could.

Those are the moves: spend less, use cash, sell sensibly, work lightly, move if necessary.

If none of them can move, the plan is brittle. If several can move, the plan may survive a much worse year than the spreadsheet originally imagined.

## Do Not Build A Bunker

Risk management has its own failure mode.

It can become a reason to postpone life forever.

There is always one more risk. Another market crash. Another policy change. Another medical scenario. Another currency worry. Another reason to work five more years so the number becomes more impressive and the anxiety has less to say.

But over-protection is also a cost.

Every extra year spent defending against a remote risk is a year not spent using the freedom the plan was built to buy. A household can make itself poorer by being reckless. It can also make itself poorer in time by refusing to accept any uncertainty.

The practical rule is this: if a risk has a lever, put the lever in the plan.

The main levers are liquidity, low fixed costs, catastrophe insurance, kept-alive skills, and a legal escape route if the household ever needs one.

If a risk has no lever, do not turn it into a shrine.

FI does not promise that the future will behave. It reduces the number of future events that can command your life.

The plan does not need to be indestructible.

It needs room to move.

## Notes

<a id="note-1"></a>[1] Chapter 4 explains the withdrawal-rate framework and cites William Bengen's 1994 paper and Robert Shiller's historical U.S. market data. This chapter uses the same idea for risk. The key issue is sequence-of-returns risk: poor market returns early in retirement can damage a withdrawal plan more than the same returns arriving later. The 4 percent, 5 percent, and 6 percent comparisons are planning intuition from historical backtests, not a guarantee that future markets will match the past.

<a id="note-2"></a>[2] National Population and Talent Division, [Population in Brief 2025](https://www.population.gov.sg/files/media-centre/publications/Population_in_Brief_2025.pdf). Figures cited include the citizen population aged 65 and above, the 2030 projection, resident total fertility rate, total non-resident population, and the foreign workforce share of the non-resident population.

<a id="note-3"></a>[3] CPF Board, [3 simple steps to make a CPF transfer from your OA to SA](https://www.cpf.gov.sg/member/infohub/educational-resources/simple-steps-to-make-a-cpf-transfer-from-your-oa-to-sa). CPF describes OA and SA uses, notes that OA-to-SA transfers can improve retirement savings, and warns that transfers are irreversible and reduce flexibility for housing needs.

<a id="note-4"></a>[4] gov.sg, [How MAS accumulates official foreign reserves](https://www.gov.sg/explainers/how-mas-accumulates-official-foreign-reserves/). The explainer describes MAS accumulating official foreign reserves when it purchases U.S. dollars in exchange for Singapore dollars to moderate appreciation pressure, and links those pressures to public-sector liquidity withdrawal and capital inflows reflecting confidence in Singapore.

<a id="note-5"></a>[5] Ministry of Health, [About Integrated Shield Plan](https://www.moh.gov.sg/managing-expenses/schemes-and-subsidies/integrated-shield-plans/about-integrated-shield-plans/). The page explains that Integrated Shield Plans are private medical insurance plans that add coverage on top of MediShield Life.

<a id="note-6"></a>[6] MoneySense, [Insurance](https://www.moneysense.gov.sg/insurance/). MoneySense frames insurance as protection against financial loss and provides basic guides to health, life, term, and other insurance types.

<a id="note-7"></a>[7] HDB, [Eligibility for Renting Out a Flat](https://www.hdb.gov.sg/managing-my-home/home-ownership/renting-out-a-flat-or-bedrooms/renting-out-a-flat/eligibility), and HDB, [Standard, Plus, and Prime Housing Framework](https://www.hdb.gov.sg/buying-a-flat/bto-sbf-and-open-booking-of-flats/finding-a-new-flat/standard-plus-and-prime-housing-framework). HDB states that Singapore Citizen flat owners who have fulfilled the Minimum Occupation Period can rent out their flats, subject to the relevant rules and approvals; under the Standard/Plus/Prime framework, whole-flat rental is allowed for Standard flats after MOP but not allowed for Plus or Prime flats.
